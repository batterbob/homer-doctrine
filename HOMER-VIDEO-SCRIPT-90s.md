# HOMER — intro video for the second operator (~100 s; trims to 90 s)

**Audience:** the second operator. **Goal:** she understands what it does, roughly how it works,
and what she gets — enough to decide if she wants it on her phone.
**Length:** ~270 words ≈ 100 s at a calm pace. 6 scenes. To hit 90 s, cut Scene 5's
second sentence and tighten Scene 1.

**SCOPE — read before producing.** What's on offer today is the household brief
delivered to her phone. Her own email is **not** read and is not part of this. Say it
once (Scene 5); don't imply otherwise anywhere.

**PRIVACY RULE FOR VISUALS:** no real names, school, teachers, grades, or real message
text. Mock everything.

---

## SCENE 1 — the problem (0:00–0:12)
**VISUAL:** Six school notifications stacking up on a phone; a permission slip buried
on a counter.
**NARRATION:**
"School information arrives in about six different places. The grade portal, email,
announcements, a flyer in a backpack. None of it's hard — there's just a lot, and
keeping track of it is its own job."

## SCENE 2 — what it does (0:12–0:32)
**VISUAL:** Those six sources flow into one funnel → a single short message.
**NARRATION:**
"So this checks all of them for us, every morning, and turns them into one message at
six a.m. What's happening today, what the kids need to bring, anything that changed,
and anything that needs one of us to decide. If there's nothing worth saying, it says
it's a quiet day — it doesn't invent things to tell you."

## SCENE 3 — how it works (0:32–1:02) ← the part that earns trust
**VISUAL:** Split screen. Left: a checklist ticking through sources, marked "fixed
rules." Right: a pen writing a sentence, marked "wording only."
**NARRATION:**
"Two halves. The first checks each source and decides what's worth mentioning — and
that half is ordinary, fixed rules. A grade dropped. A form is due Friday. It's not
an AI guessing what matters.

The second half is the AI, and its only job is writing it in plain English. It decides
nothing. So it can't invent a problem, and it can't quietly decide something isn't
worth telling you.

And it never *does* anything on its own — no replies sent, nothing changed. It
suggests; a person approves."

## SCENE 4 — what you get out of it (1:02–1:22)
**VISUAL:** A calm morning. One phone, one short message, read and put down.
**NARRATION:**
"What that buys you: you stop holding all of it in your head. Forms don't get found
the night before. You both see the same thing at the same time, so it's not one
person's job to remember. And on a normal day, it's ten seconds of reading."

## SCENE 5 — the limits, plainly (1:22–1:36)
**VISUAL:** Three quiet cards: "No scores on the kids." "Your inbox is yours."
"Off whenever you want."
**NARRATION:**
"Three things it won't do. It never scores or ranks the kids — it describes what
happened. It doesn't read your email; that's not part of this. And you can turn it off
any day, for any reason, without breaking anything else."

## SCENE 6 — close (1:36–1:44)
**VISUAL:** The single six a.m. message.
**NARRATION:**
"It's new, so it'll get things wrong sometimes — telling us when it does is how it
improves. But that's the whole idea: one message, once a morning."

**END CARD:** One message. Six a.m. That's it.

---

## PRODUCTION NOTES
- **Tone:** warm, plain, unhurried — one person explaining something to another. No
  hype, no "AI-powered," no product voice.
- **Pace:** ~150 wpm. Slow down in Scene 3; that's where trust is won or lost.
- **Visual style:** flat, uncluttered, fewer elements each scene, ending on one phone.
- **Leave out:** the agents, the audit process, the architecture, the source list by
  name. She's deciding whether she wants a morning message, not reviewing a system.
- **Stills + voiceover is enough:** 6 images, one per scene.
